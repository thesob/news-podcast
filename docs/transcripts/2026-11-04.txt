Patricio, the single thread running through today is reversal — of political power in Washington, of market positioning, of who's assumed to be in charge. And the market reaction is the part that lands closest to you in Chile. The dollar sliding to a one-month low is, on balance, friendly to Chilean terms of trade: copper is priced in dollars, so a weaker dollar tends to firm up the peso and support copper's local value. But read the reason the dollar fell — fears that a Democratic Congress means tougher AI regulation and therefore slower US growth — and the picture gets more two-sided. Slower US and global growth eventually softens demand for industrial metals, so the same story that's good for the peso today could, if it hardens into a genuine growth scare, chip away at copper demand down the line. Worth watching, not a confident prediction.
[EN]
The Strait of Hormuz is the thread I'd keep one eye on for the opposite reason. Chile imports essentially all its oil, so any sustained disruption to that chokepoint feeds straight into fuel prices and, with a lag, into local inflation — exactly the kind of imported-cost pressure that complicates the central bank's job. For now it reads as a flare-up with diplomacy running alongside it, so I wouldn't overweight it; but a tanker war in Hormuz is the sort of tail risk that doesn't announce itself politely before it moves the oil price.
[EN]
There's also a quietly interesting political mirror today. The US just swung sharply left in a midterm rebuke of its right-populist president, on the very day Chile's President Kast — who arrived via the opposite, rightward swing — was in Madrid shaking hands with Felipe VI. It's a reminder that the direction of democratic politics isn't a global tide moving everyone the same way at once; it's local, reactive, and often out of phase between countries. For a Chilean reading the world, that's a useful corrective to importing US political mood wholesale.
[EN]
Finally, the energy stories — Sweden's wild north-south electricity split and Norway's record-warm October — rhyme with a theme that matters a lot for Chile's own transition: the grid, not the generation, is becoming the binding constraint. Sweden can have cheap power in the north and punishing prices in the south at the same hour because the wires and market zones can't move it fast enough. Chile, with its superb solar in the north and demand in the center, knows this problem intimately. The lesson other countries are learning the hard way — that transmission and storage are where the real fight is — is one Chile is well-placed to get ahead of. Worth watching how that plays out.
