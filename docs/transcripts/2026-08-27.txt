Patricio, three of today's stories are really one story wearing different clothes: Nvidia buying Hugging Face for 12.9 billion dollars, Zhipu shipping a strong open-weight model the same day, and Canada and the US trading 50 percent tariffs on each other's goods. What connects them is that the infrastructure of the next decade — AI compute, AI models, and the physical trade in the metals and hardware that make both possible — is being fought over simultaneously through acquisition, open-source counter-moves, and tariff walls. None of these are separate battles; they're the same contest for who controls the chokepoints, played out in three different arenas at once.

[EN]
For you specifically, sitting in Chile, the trade-war thread is the one with the most direct pull on your wallet. Copper has reportedly been trading near record highs this week, per Chilean financial press I saw while researching today, not one of the assigned news sources, so take it as color rather than a headline, partly on exactly this kind of supply-chain anxiety — AI buildouts need copper, and a fragmenting trade order makes everyone want to lock in supply early. That's generally good news for the peso and for Chile's terms of trade in the short run, but it's a double-edged sword: the same tariff logic Trump is applying to Canada is the backdrop against which Chile is still negotiating its own exemptions, see the Chile-US story above — Chile is trying to be the well-behaved exception in a world where the exception is increasingly the rule, and Brazil's decision to fight tariffs legally rather than negotiate is a reminder that patience with that approach isn't universal.

[EN]
The Nepal-Tibet flood is worth sitting with too, not because it's Chile's disaster, but because it's a preview of a risk Chile shares. A glacier let go all at once and turned a slow-motion climate trend into a five-minute catastrophe. Chile's own water and energy security leans heavily on Andean snowpack and glaciers, and the mechanism in Nepal, sudden ice-dam failure rather than gradual melt, is exactly the kind of tail risk that doesn't show up in average-warming projections. Not a prediction, just a reason to notice that slow climate change and sudden climate disaster aren't actually in tension; the first quietly loads the gun for the second.

[EN]
And then there's the smaller, stranger story: developers responding to being replaced by AI not by protesting, but by building an open-source AI CEO as commentary. It's a joke, but it's also a genuine artifact of collaborative, distributed pushback — a group of people with no formal authority using open tooling to talk back to concentrated corporate power. Whether that impulse scales into anything larger is an open question, but it's a small, real data point for the kind of shift the Hypothesis Watch below is tracking.

Hypothesis Watch.

[EN]
Ongoing daily scan of your standing hypothesis about AI, authority cycles, and social cohesion — not a full re-argument each day, just what today's stories add.

[EN]
The hypothesis, broken into sub-claims, for the first time only: (a) the rise of AI is shrinking how long anyone can hold the role of thought leader or central authority; (b) that pushes individuals to develop a stronger internal voice; (c) which in turn moves people away from individualism; (d) leading to renewed human collaboration and, eventually, greater social cohesion.

[EN]
Today leans mildly toward (a) and (d), with one genuine complication. On (d): the OpenExecutive story, engineers responding to AI-driven layoffs by collaboratively open-sourcing a mocking AI CEO, is a small but concrete instance of distributed, voluntary collaboration as a response to concentrated power, exactly the pattern (d) predicts. GLM-5.3-Flash landing the same week as Nvidia's Hugging Face acquisition is similar: an open, community-oriented release keeping pace with a 12.9 billion dollar consolidation move is evidence that authority in AI isn't fully concentrating even as the biggest deal of the year says otherwise — which is itself the complication. A 12.9 billion dollar acquisition of the internet's most important open-model hub is concentration, not fragmentation, and it's a real data point against (a) if the acquirer starts steering what open means. Sweden's politics also cut against a clean story: Andersson's vague comeback bid and the Liberals' near-death experience are consistent with (a)'s shrinking authority cycles, but there's no sign yet of Swedish voters responding with (b) or (c) — party fragmentation there looks more like standard multiparty churn than a citizenry visibly building a stronger internal voice. No strong signal either way on (b) or (c) specifically today.
