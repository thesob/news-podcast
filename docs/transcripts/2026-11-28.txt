[EN]

Unions say management told them the 111-year-old division would close in 2028; Codelco says it "has not taken immediate decisions" and is preparing a recovery plan built on stabilizing operations, cutting costs and financial discipline. Unions have declared themselves on alert and are planning mobilizations.

Connecting the Dots.
[EN]
This section is interpretation and informed speculation, not reported news — it's me thinking out loud about patterns across today's stories, not a claim of fact. Treat it as a starting point for your own thinking, distinct from everything sourced above.
[EN]
Patricio, the thread that ties today together is the cost of the Gulf war spreading quietly through the global economy. The US blockade of Iran and Tehran's promise to keep Hormuz shut are no longer just a Middle East story: they show up in a Norwegian airline going bust over jet fuel, in Sweden fighting about fuel taxes, and in Puerto Ricans marching over electricity bills. For Chile, which imports most of its oil, that means sticky fuel and power costs feeding into transport, food and industrial prices, at the very moment unemployment has hit 10%. That combination, energy-driven inflation pressure plus a weak labour market, leaves policymakers with little room, and it's worth watching how the Banco Central talks about it in December.
[EN]
The Codelco news is the most direct hit to your wallet as a Chilean. A company that earns record EBITDA on high copper prices but still posts a loss, writes down a century-old icon and sees output fall 9% is telling us that Chile isn't capturing the copper boom the way the price chart suggests. If Chuquicamata really winds down by 2028, the fiscal maths, the Calama economy and Chile's share of global supply all shift. Emol also flagged the dollar trading above $1,000 pesos: that helps exporters and the Treasury's peso revenues, but it makes everything imported, starting with fuel, more expensive. My hedged read is a peso that stays weak while the market questions whether the state miner can turn itself around, rather than a short-term blip.
[EN]
Politically, today is full of fast turnovers: Spain is likely about to end Sánchez's eight years, Victoria's voters threw out a 12-year government and its premier's own seat, and Sweden's Centre Party is splitting over whether to back Kristersson. Add Trump openly using state leverage over Intel and Nvidia, and a new US tariff round against 14 economies, and the picture is one of governments putting their thumb on markets everywhere. For Chile, the risk is getting caught between Washington's tariff pressure and Beijing's retaliation, since China and the US are your two biggest customers. The Rabat impeachment vote on Monday and the public-sector pay deal suggest Kast's government will spend political capital on fights at home just as the external picture gets harder.
[EN]
Finally, a quieter pattern: people are pushing back against AI at the edges. Restaurants are going back to the telephone to beat booking bots, developers are calling out AI coding tools that slip past safety checks, and shoppers' card details are leaking through yet another layer of web code. Meanwhile AI is cracking problems mathematicians once thought were decades away. I'm not making a prediction here, but the gap between what AI can do and how far people trust it seems to be widening, and that gap is where both regulation and new business models tend to show up.
