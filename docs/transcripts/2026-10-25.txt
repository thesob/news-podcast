Patricio, the single thread that ties today most tightly to your doorstep in Chile is oil at 115 dollars. That number doesn't stay abstract for you: it props up a weak peso that's already over 1,000 to the dollar, feeds domestic inflation, and raises the cost of everything imported and fuel-driven. The one offsetting force is copper — a jittery, conflict-driven commodity cycle that lifts oil often lifts industrial metals too, and a firmer copper price is the natural hedge for Chile's terms of trade. Worth watching this week: whether copper is riding the same risk-premium wave or whether Chile is simply importing expensive energy while its export anchor lags. If it's the latter, the peso pressure and the Fondo E anxiety start to rhyme in an uncomfortable way.

Either way, a near fifty-fifty result guarantees a bitterly divided Brazil next door — and divided big neighbors tend to export volatility, not calm.

One hundred and seventy-plus demonstrations across Europe in a month, France arresting a thousand people in a day, Tirana clashing — this isn't about one grievance, it's a broad cost-of-living and legitimacy strain in wealthy democracies. Chile has lived its own version of this since 2019, so you know the pattern: when the economic squeeze — hello, 115 dollar oil and a sagging peso — meets a sense that institutions aren't delivering, the street fills up. Not a prediction, just a weather system worth tracking.

The honest read is that we're entering the disillusionment-and-reckoning phase of the cycle, where the interesting question shifts from what can it do to what did it actually cost, and who's left holding judgment.
