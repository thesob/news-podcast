Patricio, the thread I'd pull on first today is the collision between two kinds of pressure on sovereign budgets. The UK's 30-year borrowing costs hitting a 28-year high isn't a lonely British problem — it's the sharp end of a global bond sell-off, and bond markets tend to move in sympathy. When the cost of long money rises in London, it rarely stays in London. For a copper-and-lithium economy like Chile's, already contracting one percent in August with mining at a 30-year low, tighter and pricier global capital is exactly the headwind you don't want layered on top of a domestic downturn. The 2027 budget fight in Santiago — a government defending one and a half percent spending growth while the opposition disputes even how that's measured — is being fought in a room where the external walls are closing in.
[EN]
That connects to copper in a way worth watching. A weak global growth signal — bond stress, France in turmoil, war grinding on — usually weighs on industrial-metal demand, and copper is Chile's fiscal heartbeat. But the same AI-and-electrification story running through the tech section — orbital-compute fantasies, data-center build-outs, geothermal milestones — is a structural bid under copper over the medium term. So you're caught between a cyclical drag, a wobbly world economy, and a secular pull, everything electric needing wire. I wouldn't bet confidently either way on the peso or the copper price from one day's news, but the tension between those two forces is the story to keep half an eye on.
[EN]
The third pattern is softer but real: institutions under strain, from different directions. Trump officials quietly standing down election-threat investigations, Tennessee's execution apparatus failing twice, France closing 400 schools under protest, Ethiopia and Eritrea severing ties — these are all, in their way, stories about the machinery of the state stuttering. In Chile the echo is domestic: a contested budget, a corruption arrest in Gendarmería, disputed crowd counts at the Confech march. None of these are the same event, but together they sketch a year in which the credibility of official institutions is being tested nearly everywhere, and where citizens increasingly arbitrate the facts themselves.
[EN]
Finally, the AI governance drumbeat — OpenAI shedding safety researchers, chatbots caught stripping hijabs, regulators fining robotaxis — keeps pointing at the same gap: the technology is moving faster than anyone's ability to hold it accountable. For you, sitting in Chile, the practical read is that the rules shaping AI are being written in Washington, Brussels and a handful of boardrooms, and Chile will mostly be a rule-taker. Worth watching whether Latin American governments start asserting their own line, the way the EU has — but nothing today suggests that's imminent.
