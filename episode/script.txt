Connecting the Dots.
[EN]
This section is interpretation and informed speculation, not reported news — it's me thinking out loud about patterns across today's stories, not a claim of fact. Treat it as a starting point for your own thinking, distinct from everything sourced above.
[EN]
Patricio, the thread I keep seeing today is energy and war costs reaching ordinary household budgets. The Iran conflict is now killing foreign workers in Kuwait, Europe's inflation is climbing again on energy, southern Sweden faces its most expensive electricity bill in four years, and Iran itself is cutting bread and petrol subsidies. When the Gulf is this unsettled, oil and gas prices tend to stay high and volatile. For Chile, which imports most of its fuel, that pressure arrives directly, at the petrol pump and in electricity contracts.
[EN]
The second thread is the peso, and it's the one most worth watching from Santiago. A dollar around $1,040–1,050 CLP, with JP Morgan talking about $1,200 by mid-2027, combines a strong dollar and expected Fed hikes with falling copper prices and weak local activity. The Congo story makes it worse: Chile's copper shipments are down 15% this year while Congo, largely through Chinese-owned mines, closes the gap. Over the long run, copper demand from AI data centres and grids should stay strong (SpaceX's million-satellite plan and Nvidia's chip politics are reminders of how much physical infrastructure AI needs). The real question is whether Chile captures that demand or loses ground to Congo. For your own finances, a weaker peso makes imports, travel and anything priced in dollars more expensive. This isn't a confident forecast, but it's a reason to think about how much of your exposure is in pesos.
[EN]
Third, the politics. Spain looks likely to move right on Sunday, with Vox possibly entering government. Sweden's Centre Party just split over joining a right-leaning government, and New Zealand ended up with a hung parliament. The EU couldn't agree on a budget or on Ukraine's loan. Spain is one of Chile's biggest investors, so a change of government in Madrid matters for Spanish banks, utilities and infrastructure firms operating in Chile, though probably more in tone than in substance at first. Closer to home, Lula appointing a banker as finance minister drew a 6% rally in São Paulo, which suggests that in this climate markets reward governments that signal fiscal discipline. That's a dynamic Kast's government will recognise.
[EN]
Finally, a quieter signal: Chile's competition regulator going after Apple for $80M+ fits a global pattern of mid-sized countries asserting rules over tech giants, as Brazil and the EU already have. Put that next to Nvidia now being policed through Southeast Asian supply chains, and the picture is a tech world getting more regulated and more geopolitical. Chile could benefit as a "neutral" host for data centres and treaties (Kast is also pushing Valparaíso as host of the High Seas Treaty secretariat), but that's an opportunity to watch, not something already won.
