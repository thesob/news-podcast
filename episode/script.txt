Patricio, the loudest pattern today is a world being re-sorted by its biggest players while everyone else watches from the waiting room. At the same time, Washington is tightening chip curbs and Nvidia is walking away from China, so the tech map is splitting even as leaders smile on the tarmac. For Chile, which sells copper and cherries to China and buys security and capital from the West, that is the classic squeeze: a summit that "stabilises" US–China ties is good news for copper demand, but any deal on Ukraine or tech that is cut over the heads of mid-sized countries is worth watching closely.

[EN]

Iran's strike on Kuwait after the US hit Qeshm Island, more interceptions over Bahrain and nervous shipping in Hormuz are the kind of headlines that feed straight into fuel prices — and you can already see the downstream: UK bills forecast up 26%, Germany cutting electricity taxes, and Chile freezing fuel prices until April 2027. That freeze is why La Tercera expects Chilean inflation to end the year under 5%, but it shifts the bill to the Treasury just as the opposition is attacking Quiroz over a US$160 million pension-fund gap. My not-confident read: if the Gulf flare-up lasts into the southern summer, Chile's fiscal room, not pump prices, becomes the pressure point.

[EN]
Third, money is getting jittery in our neighbourhood. Brazil's real hit its weakest level since April 2025 on doubts about Lula's fiscal plan, and Chile's pension funds have now booked almost US$7 billion in realised losses on currency swaps. Those swaps exist to hedge dollar exposure, so they are a reminder that peso volatility hits ordinary retirement savings, not just importers. If emerging-market currencies wobble further while copper stays supported by the AI build-out (Nvidia's US$115.8bn quarter is, in the end, a story about data centres made of copper), the peso could be pulled in two directions at once.

[EN]
Finally, a quieter but important thread: digital fragility. A week of US outages, Java4Shell found with AI help, the Optimizely malware hitting 178 major sites, Equinor under attack, and Santiago's Hospital Clínico unable to book appointments. Rubio's suggestion that the rush to patch AI-exposed bugs may itself be breaking systems is plausible — AI is finding holes faster than old infrastructure can be safely fixed. Chile just signed a non-binding "OpenAI for Chile" memorandum to put AI into the state; it might be worth asking, as a citizen, that resilience and backup plans get the same attention as the shiny tools.
