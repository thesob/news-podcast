Patricio, the thread running through today is that confidence in the AI build-out is starting to wobble. The wobble is now reaching places that seem a long way from Silicon Valley. Nvidia's CEO drama has turned into a governance question that S&P is putting a price on. Because Nvidia guarantees so much of the industry's infrastructure debt, a weaker credit story for Nvidia is a weaker story for the whole data-center pipeline. At the same time, the technology itself keeps proving extraordinary: if Aaronson's account holds up, AI is closing open problems faster than people can digest them. So the market is pricing a strange combination: huge capability alongside real doubt about who captures the money and whether today's spending pays back on time.
[EN]
That's where Chile comes in. Copper was the "AI metal" of 2026, needed for grids and data centers, and it has now given back the whole year's gains in twelve sessions. The peso is at a record low near 1,050 to the dollar. For you, that means imported goods, fuel and anything priced in dollars (electronics, travel, many Chilean mortgages and contracts indexed to costs) gets more expensive. Quiroz is right to worry about inflation. It's worth watching whether Costa keeps her hands off the currency if the slide continues; so far she sees it as a healthy shock absorber.
[EN]
The new US tariffs add a second layer. If China and Southeast Asian producers are pushed out of the US market, their surplus has to go somewhere. Latin America is an obvious destination, which is good for Chilean consumers but hard on local manufacturers. A trade war that slows China is also usually bad news for copper demand. Add a US electorate that just punished high prices at the midterms, and Washington may be heading for a messy, inflationary 2027. The world's biggest economy can export that volatility to commodity-dependent countries like Chile.
[EN]
Two quieter counterweights stand out. Chile passed Sala Cuna Universal with credit openly shared across three governments, a rare bit of cross-partisan construction while politics elsewhere is polarized. And the EU, despite its budget brawl, keeps trying to fund Ukraine together. Spain's election on Sunday matters to you too: Spain is Chile's main European political and investment partner, and a PP–Vox government would probably change Madrid's tone on migration and on Latin America. None of this is a confident prediction. The pattern worth watching is AI-driven market swings feeding straight into Chile's currency and copper revenue, while governments try, with mixed success, to build things that last.
