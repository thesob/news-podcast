Patricio, the single loudest signal today is that energy has become an instrument of war, and that lands squarely on Chile. With Bab el-Mandeb and Hormuz both contested and Brent back at 100 dollars, the country that imports almost all of its oil and diesel is about to feel it at the pump, in freight costs, and in a peso that tends to weaken when the dollar and oil rise together. But there's a genuine two-sidedness here worth holding in your head: the same instability that raises your fuel-import bill also puts a risk premium under commodities broadly, and copper is the one that matters for Chile's fiscal accounts. If the war narrative deepens without crushing global growth, a firmer copper price could partly offset the energy hit. That tug-of-war, energy import bill versus copper windfall, is the thing to actually watch over the next few weeks, not either headline alone.

The second thread is the slow shattering of the trade system you and every open commodity economy depend on. Trump's 50 percent tariffs and outright import bans on Canada aren't about Chile, but they're a template, and they signal a world where supply chains re-route around politics rather than efficiency. Chile's comparative advantage has always been being a reliable, relatively neutral supplier of things everyone needs; that's an asset in a fragmenting world, but it only pays off if global demand holds. A tariff-driven slowdown in the US and its trading partners is the tail risk that would hit copper demand right when you'd most want it high, the opposite side of the coin from the energy story above.
