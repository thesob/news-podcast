[EN]

Patricio, the thread I can't stop pulling today is fragility. In a single day the US air traffic system fell over because new tech was bolted onto old switches, Microsoft's cloud went down hard enough to silence courts and hospitals, and the most powerful executive in AI was gone within a week of one letter. None of these is a collapse on its own, but together they hint that the systems we lean on are more concentrated, and more brittle, than they look. Worth watching whether "resilience" becomes the next big spending theme the way "AI" was this year.
[EN]
The energy picture is the one that lands closest to home. Four ships hit near Hormuz in a day is exactly the kind of thing that keeps oil prices jumpy, and Chile's answer this week is a fuel-price freeze through February plus diesel relief for the truckers. That buys calm at the pump, but someone pays for it, likely the fiscal accounts, and it locks in a political deadline right in the middle of summer. If Rubio is right that Washington is done escalating, the pressure could ease; if the mines keep going off, the freeze gets more expensive to maintain.
[EN]
Then there's the big-power theatre in Shenzhen. A Trump–Xi–Putin table, Kast heading to Asia, CEPAL talking up Chinese cooperation in Santiago, and a fresh tariff threat against Mexico all point the same way: Latin America is being asked, more bluntly, to pick lanes. For a copper exporter, US–China détente is generally good news (calmer demand, steadier peso), while a breakdown would bite. The Nvidia story ties in too: AI data centres eat copper, and anything that slows AI investment, a funding gap or tighter chip controls, ripples down to Chilean mines eventually. Not a prediction, just the transmission belt to keep in mind.
[EN]
Finally, the politics. Spain heads to the polls on 29-N with the right reportedly near 190 seats, Sweden is still assembling a government, and young American voters lurched left in the midterms. Spanish banks, utilities and builders are big players in Chile, so a policy shift in Madrid can matter here more than it seems. And the pattern of electorates swinging hard, fast and in different directions in different places is itself the story: mandates feel shorter-lived than they used to.
